# Style and Gear Proxies

Style prompts are more actionable when they describe observable sound
properties rather than relying on a single artist or genre label. A useful
proxy combines musical era, arrangement, instrument character, performance,
recording, and mix language. These dimensions help communicate a target while
leaving room for an original result.

## Deconstruct the sound

Translate a reference impression into independent cues:

- **Era and production context:** for example, late-1970s studio warmth,
  early-2000s digital polish, or contemporary lo-fi bedroom recording.
- **Arrangement:** sparse trio, layered synthesizers, orchestral swells, or
  call-and-response vocals.
- **Instrument character:** muted electric piano, rounded synth bass, brushed
  drums, bright picked strings, or an overdriven guitar.
- **Performance:** clipped and precise, loose and swung, intimate, forceful, or
  softly articulated.
- **Recording and mix:** close-miked, tape-like saturation, restrained
  compression, wide backing layers, dry percussion, or a long vocal tail.
- **Mood and dynamics:** tense, wistful, buoyant, restrained, or gradually
  building.

Use concrete combinations rather than adjective lists. "Warm" can refer to
timbre, harmony, or mix; pair it with the sound source and intended effect.

## Describe gear as a proxy

Gear terms can suggest a family of timbres, but a text prompt does not select
specific hardware unless a system exposes that control. Prefer descriptions
such as "rounded, gently saturated analog-style bass with a slow attack" over
a model-specific promise that a named synthesizer will appear. Similarly,
"short spring-like reverb" communicates a spatial character, not a guaranteed
hardware emulation.

Mixing words should identify the target and relationship: a dry vocal in front
of a hazy backing texture; narrow low frequencies under wide upper harmonies;
or soft compression on the bus while percussion remains transient. Avoid
contradictory requests such as "very dry" and "long, cavernous reverb" for the
same source unless the distinction is explicit.

## Use era cues carefully

Era labels can invoke common production conventions, not precise historical
accuracy. Support an era with a few relevant details, such as drum-machine
character, stereo width, instrument palette, and vocal treatment. Avoid
stacking incompatible decades or production aesthetics without describing how
they should be combined.

## Example transformation

Instead of a prompt that depends on a direct artist imitation, describe the
desired qualities:

> Intimate late-night art-pop, slow syncopated pulse, soft electric-piano
> chords, rounded bass, restrained analog-style synth textures, close dry lead
> vocal, and a wide reverberant response in the phrase endings. Keep the
> arrangement sparse in the verses and add layered harmony in the chorus.

This gives a model multiple interpretable dimensions and helps the writer
adjust one dimension at a time. The resulting sound remains model-dependent;
style and gear words are conditioning cues, not a guarantee of exact timbre.
